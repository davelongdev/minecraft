# Turn Game Forge into a kids' RPG

The most-played things inside Minecraft are a persistent RPG-like world (Hypixel SkyBlock, about **61% of Hypixel's players** in a January 2025 snapshot) and a short list of round-based minigames led by **Bed Wars**, then SkyWars, Duels, Murder Mystery, Hide and Seek, Build Battle, and the MCC-style party games. That split is the design brief for Game Forge: kids come back for a character that grows, and they play in short, readable rounds. Every RPG system those servers share (classes, levelled skills, an unlock tree, collections, quests from NPCs, rarity-tiered gear, dice, a menu item) has a working vanilla 26.3 equivalent, and the repo already contains most of the plumbing: per-player ids and storage, dialogs, advancement tabs, `/trigger` menus, the Magic Compass, and NPC Forge's characters. Kids' tabletop RPGs show how to translate the D&D side for ages 8–14: d6 dice and "is it higher?" checks, tokens you spend instead of numbers you write down, symbols on the sheet, short sessions, and a built-in "too scary" signal. The motivation research sets the one hard rule: **don't pay kids in loot for doing lessons**. Expected, completion-contingent rewards measurably undermine interest, especially in children, and leaderboards coincided with falling motivation in a classroom trial. So learning should unlock *abilities and building tools* used in play, rewards should be cosmetic, surprising, or verbal, and progress should be cooperative (houses, class totals) rather than ranked.

## Bed Wars and SkyBlock set the template kids already know

On Java, Hypixel is the reference point. A forum snapshot from January 12, 2025 put **SkyBlock at about 27,534 concurrent players (about 61% of the network) and Bed Wars at about 5,879 (about 13%)** ([Hypixel forums](https://hypixel.net/threads/how-much-each-game-contributes-to-hypixels-player-count-january-12-2025.5834698/)); a mid-2025 community estimate said Bed Wars holds about 70% of the non-SkyBlock PvP players ([Hypixel forums](https://hypixel.net/threads/is-skyblock-hard-carrying-hypixel-player-counts.5961889/)). Both are community counts seen only as search snippets, not averages, but they agree on the shape. Wikipedia describes SkyBlock as a mode that "functions more like an MMORPG" and lists Bed Wars and SkyWars as Hypixel's other headline games ([Wikipedia: Hypixel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypixel)); a Sportskeeda ranking puts Murder Mystery, Duels, and SkyWars at 3rd to 5th ([Sportskeeda](https://sportskeeda.com/minecraft/5-popular-minecraft-games-hypixel-server)).

Younger kids are more likely to know Bedrock's **The Hive**, which claims **7M+ monthly players** and swapped its Treasure Wars mode for BedWars in March 2024 ([MinecraftPal](https://www.minecraftpal.com/news/91/how-the-hive-became-minecrafts-biggest-server-by-killing-its-own-java-edition)). Its headline modes are Hide and Seek (hiders disguised as blocks), Murder Mystery, DeathRun, Just Build, SkyWars, Block Drop, and Ground Wars ([GGServers](https://ggservers.com/blog/top-10-minecraft-bedrock-edition-servers-in-2025/); [Hive Support](https://support.playhive.com/gamemodes)). The Hive is a featured Bedrock server, which makes it one of the only servers console players can reach at all. The creator event **MC Championship** and its server MCC Island set the trend for team party games: ten teams of four play eight short games for coins, then the top two meet in Dodgebolt, an archery duel ([Wikipedia: MC Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MC_Championship)). Its pool (Battle Box, Hole in the Wall, Parkour Warrior, Sky Battle, Rocket Spleef, Build Mart) is a ready menu of formats that fit a class. A cross-server snapshot also shows the persistent genres: Prison economies, Lifesteal and Towny survival, and **Wynncraft, a full MMORPG at about 9,000 concurrent** ([BlockBase Tools](https://blockbase-tools.com/statistics/top-minecraft-servers/), undated third-party figures, rough at best).

Read together, the evidence ranks round-based games roughly as Bed Wars, SkyWars, Duels, Murder Mystery, Hide and Seek, Build Battle, Survival Games, Parkour and DeathRun, the Spleef family, and Capture the Flag, with SkyBlock and RPG questing leading the long-form side. Per-mode counts for The Hive, CubeCraft, and MCC Island don't exist publicly, so the order below the top two is an inference.

## Short rounds fit a class of 10–20; long PvP needs a teacher's eye

Popularity and classroom fit aren't the same thing. Bed Wars runs 15–30 minutes, punishes the weakest players hardest, and invites spawn-camping, so it needs shortening and supervision. The modes that suit a mixed 8–14 group are the ones where an eliminated kid is back in within a minute. The table below rates each against vanilla 26.3 (scoreboards, teams, `/trigger`, dialogs, `place template`, `fill`/`clone`, attribute modifiers, item components). Round lengths are typical values, not sourced figures, and the feasibility ratings are a judgement informed by the repo's own `pvp-arena`, `one-block`, and `tile-game` packs.

| Mode | Core loop | Vanilla build | Class fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| TNT Run / Spleef / Block Drop | Floor vanishes; last one standing | Easy: per-tick `setblock ~ ~-1 ~ air`, `fill` to reset | Ideal first project, 1–3 min rounds |
| Parkour / Dropper | Timed course, checkpoints | Easy: one-block_2's speedrun timer is reusable | Personal bests, no losers |
| Hole in the Wall | Walls slide across a platform | Easy-medium: `clone` a step at a time | Readable and funny |
| Battle Box / King of the Hill | Hold or fill a zone with team blocks | Easy-medium: `execute if blocks` | Several small arenas at once |
| Build Battle | Themed timed build, then a vote | Easy-medium: `build-plots` plus a `/trigger` vote | Strongest educational fit; teacher approves builds |
| Deathrun | One player triggers traps on runners | Medium: buttons → command blocks | Teaches Command Forge section 2 |
| Murder Mystery | Secret murderer, detective, innocents | Medium: random role, hidden name tags | Social deduction; moderate the chat |
| Capture the Flag / The Bridge | Steal and carry, or score in a goal | Medium: banner with `custom_data`, zone checks | Teamwork and roles |
| Bed Wars | Generators, shop, defend your bed | Medium-hard: timers, shop dialog, respawn flag | Too long unless trimmed |
| Hide and Seek (prop) | Hiders disguised as blocks | Medium-hard: invisible player plus a `block_display` | Plain hide and seek is easy |
| Tower defense / Escape room | Waves on a path; clue puzzles | Medium / easy-medium | Co-op; strategy, logic, reading |

Minecraft Education's own esports material centres on **team build battles**, with lesson plans aimed at creativity, decision-making, and collaboration ([Minecraft Education blog](https://education.minecraft.net/blog/explore-esports-with-new-minecraft-education-edition-lessons-and-teaching-guide)), and its 45-minute "Code 2 Create: Gold Rush" wraps coding in a build battle with peer assessment and teacher-led reflection ([Minecraft Education](https://education.minecraft.net/fi-fi/lessons/code-2-create-gold-rush2)). On safety, government parent guidance says the server, not the mode, decides it: public server admins don't have to moderate chat, so kids should play on a server run by a trusted adult ([NSW school fact sheet](https://castlehill-p.schools.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/doe/sws/schools/c/castlehill-p/cybersafety/MinecraftParents_FA_web.pdf)). Dave's whitelisted studio box already meets that bar, which leaves the in-mode risks: inappropriate builds, accusation chat in hidden-role games, and older kids dominating younger ones in PvP. Factions, Lifesteal, and anarchy formats don't belong in an 8–14 program.

Each popular mode also maps onto a board-game family the kids can design on paper first: Bed Wars is a resource-and-economy game, Murder Mystery is Werewolf, CTF is a territory game, and Prison is an economics lesson about prices and inflation. That pairing lets a kid's analog design become a specific Minecraft build.

## Kids' tabletop RPGs translate as dice, tokens, and symbols

Kids' RPGs keep D&D's core loop (a character, a goal, a roll, a choice) and cut the arithmetic, the reading, and the GM's sole authority. **Hero Kids** (ages about 4–10, 30–60 minute sessions) swaps the d20 for d6s: attacker and defender each roll one to three dice and compare their highest, so the only math is "which is bigger", and its sheets put a shield next to defence and a sword next to attack ([Engaged Family Gaming](https://engagedfamilygaming.com/tabletop-rpg-review-hero-kids/)). **No Thank You, Evil!** was designed for kids with reading or math difficulties, colour blindness, and autism ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/articles/news/view/31591/more-details-monte-cooks-kids-rpg)). Tasks run on a 1–6 scale, and kids spend Smart, Tough, or Fast tokens to lower the target by one or an Awesome token to reroll ([Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/no-thank-you-evil-review-a-role-playing-game-for-kids/)). Its namesake token lets any player say the game is too intense and ask for a break ([Monte Cook Games](https://www.montecookgames.com/no-thank-you-evil-whats-in-the-box/)). **Kids on Bikes** builds the town together from sentence starters ("Our town is famous for…"), and its GM acts more as a moderator picking which player-made threads to follow ([Gnome Stew](https://gnomestew.com/take-me-to-the-upside-down-a-review-of-kids-on-bikes/)).

Each of those ideas has a direct Minecraft form, and several already exist in the repo:

| Tabletop idea | Minecraft 26.3 form | Already in the repo |
|---|---|---|
| d6 roll vs a target 1–6 | `execute store result score @s roll run random value 1..6`, then `if score … matches` | Command Forge section 2 quest 5, "Roll the Dice" |
| Highest die wins (Hero Kids) | Roll 1–3 times into scores, keep the max | Find-max loop in `___top` / `tut_work` |
| Spendable trait tokens | Named items or a score spent from a dialog button via `/trigger` | `tut_btn`, `npc_choice` triggers |
| Symbol character sheet | Dialog with item icons and colours, or a written book | Little Command Book, NPC Forge Book |
| "No thank you" signal | `/trigger pause` that freezes an encounter and pings the teacher | Dave's trigger-word convention |
| Shared world-building | One-box dialog form: "Our island is famous for…", stored and shown on signs | `choose_name` form, `post_sign` |
| GM-run one-shot | Teacher `___` functions for live story beats | `___reset_player`, `___fix_island` |

Free tools back this up: Wizards of the Coast gives schools and libraries a D&D Afterschool Kit with a starter set, club guidelines, and a quick DM guide ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/articles/news/view/52194/wizards-coast-announces-d-d-afterschool-kit-themed-teaching-materials)), and the "Roll for Adventure!" lessons tie character creation to writing, mapping to social studies, and dice to probability ([YMI Classroom](https://ymiclassroom.com/lesson-plans/rollforadventure/)). The evidence for which simplification matters most at which age is reviews and designer statements, not playtest data.

## Every core RPG system has a vanilla 26.3 building block

The big RPG servers run on proprietary plugins and server resource packs, so only their designs can be copied, not their code. Those designs converge. **Wynncraft** has five classes, two skill points per level spent across five stats, and an ability tree where each node must connect to one you already own, with resets only in a city or for a fee ([Wynncraft Wiki: Class](https://wynncraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Class); [Ability Tree](https://wynncraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Ability_Tree)). **Hypixel SkyBlock** has thirteen skills that level automatically from the matching action, each level paying out a permanent stat ([SkyBlock Wiki: Skills](https://hypixelskyblock.minecraft.wiki/w/Skills)), plus collections that unlock recipes at gathering milestones and rarity-tiered pets that level alongside you ([SkyBlock Wiki: Pets](https://hypixelskyblock.minecraft.wiki/w/Pets)). The one fully vanilla example found, the **Aeternum** data pack, proves the stack works without mods: 16 classes, abilities learned from scrolls, an un-droppable "ability wheel" item you right-click to cast and swap with F, regenerating mana, and class-specific quest merchants ([Aeternum on Modrinth](https://modrinth.com/mod/aeternum-rpg)). It targets 1.21.2–1.21.4, so its JSON would need porting.

The common core is a class choice, action-based skills that pay stats, a gated unlock tree, collections, rarity tiers, NPC quests, a currency, and **one menu item as the entry to everything**. Game Forge already has that last piece in the Magic Compass. The rest maps onto 26.3 like this (identifiers checked in the local 26.3 client jar unless a source is given):

| RPG system | Vanilla 26.3 tool | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Classes and stats | `/attribute … modifier add <id> <value> add_value` on pick, `remove` on respec ([minecraft.wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Commands/attribute)); `attribute_modifiers` on gear | jump-game-2's `jump_strength` boost is the precedent |
| Levels and XP | Scoreboards fed by advancement triggers (`player_killed_entity`, `consume_item`, `placed_block`, `fishing_rod_hooked`, `villager_trade`) | Cheaper than tick polling |
| Skill tree | Advancement tab with `parent` links, `impossible` nodes granted by function, `rewards.function` | NPC Forge's "Island Builder" tab does this now |
| Quests and quest log | Dialogs (`notice`, `multi_action`, `dialog_list`) plus per-progress written books | Command Forge's `start/tick/check` engine |
| Custom gear and spells | Components: `custom_data`, `item_model`, `rarity`, `lore`, `use_cooldown`, `consumable`, `equippable`, `death_protection` ([minecraft.wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Data_component_format)) | `use_cooldown` gives a native cooldown overlay |
| Ability "on hit" effects | Data-driven enchantments with `post_attack` and `run_function` | Exact `run_function` layout not yet read |
| Loot and rarity | Loot tables with `weight`, `set_components`, `enchant_randomly`; `/loot give` | 26.3 keys are `modifier`/`condition`/`type` |
| Dice and formulas | `/random value 1..20` ([minecraft.wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Commands/random)); 26.3's `/compute` ([minecraft.wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Java_Edition_26.3)) | `/compute` doesn't exist in 26.2 |
| Bosses and dungeons | `/summon`, `bossbar`, `/damage`, `/swing`, `place template`, `trial_spawner` configs | Keep custom mobs few and tagged |
| Parties and houses | Teams, team prefixes, `sidebar.team.<colour>` | Game Forge's per-dimension sidebars |
| Persistent character | Permanent id score plus a list in command storage, read via macros | `npc_pid`, `ob_id`; Game Forge is one world |

Two traps matter more than any missing feature. First, **26.3 loot tables use new JSON keys**: vanilla's files contain zero `functions` or `conditions` and over a thousand `modifier` and `condition` entries, so every pre-26 tutorial's loot JSON fails to load, and misode's generator must be set to 26.3. Second, the repo has already learned that only numeric `/trigger` payloads avoid the "Confirm Command Execution" screen. RPG menus therefore work best as dialog buttons and dropdowns with digit option ids through `/trigger` (point-buy, shop picks, class choice), with free text kept to the few forms that need it. On performance, the standing advice is advancement-triggered functions instead of per-tick checks, `type=` in every `@e`, and no per-tick NBT reads ([Planet Minecraft forum](https://www.planetminecraft.com/forums/minecraft/datapacks/datapacks-started-lagging-618305/)); one mod author measured vanilla's `inventory_changed` trigger at about 20% of tick time on a test server ([Icterine](https://modrinth.com/mod/icterine)), so use it sparingly. No current 26.x benchmarks exist.

## Make learning the key to new powers, not the paycheck

The research on classroom RPG layers is consistent: engagement goes up, learning and motivation gains are small or mixed, and the wrong reward structure backfires. A meta-analysis of gamification found small positive effects on cognitive (**g = .49**), motivational (**g = .36**), and behavioural (**g = .25**) outcomes, with only the cognitive effect holding up in the most rigorous studies, and game fiction and social interaction among the moderators ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109056/109056.pdf)). Classcraft, the best-known classroom RPG (over 30,000 teachers by a 2023 federal grant's count, and still awaiting its first randomized trial), raised time on task in one high-school physics study without changing motivation or summative scores ([IES](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/initial-efficacy-study-classcraft-gamified-approach-classroom-management); [Montana State](https://scholarworks.montana.edu/handle/1/15692)). The warning signs are sharper. A 128-study meta-analysis found that engagement-, completion-, and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice interest (**d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28**), that tangible rewards hurt children more than college students, and that **positive feedback raised interest (d = 0.33)** ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/deci%201999.pdf)); the finding is contested ([Cameron et al. 2001](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/:slug/9460423c-1ed9-4760-995d-5dd57e6628aa)), but no one disputes that announced prizes are the riskiest form. In a 16-week course with about 80 students, the section with badges and a leaderboard lost motivation and satisfaction over time, and lower intrinsic motivation tracked lower exam scores ([UCLA SMHP summary of Hanus & Fox 2015](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)). Prodigy, the commercial math RPG, drew an FTC complaint over its efficacy claims and criticism for in-game shopping and membership upsells that pull time away from the task ([EdWeek](https://edweek.org/technology/popular-interactive-math-game-prodigy-is-target-of-complaint-to-federal-trade-commission/2021/02)).

For Game Forge, "finish a Command Forge quest, get a diamond sword for the arena" is exactly the completion-contingent, expected, tangible reward that undermined interest most. The safer design makes the learning itself the power. A kid who finishes the `/tp` quest earns a Waystone that teleports between hub landmarks; finishing the command-block quests unlocks a trap-builder kit for designing a Deathrun room; making three NPCs already unlocks the Island Button. The reward is competence the kid can use, and it pays off in building and exploring, not in beating another kid. In the PvP arena everyone keeps the same kit, as `pvp-arena` already does, so classes stay playstyles (Builder, Coder, Explorer, Storyteller) with cosmetic or team-helpful perks, never a combat edge. Cosmetics (team-prefix titles like the existing medal stars, particle trails, a pet that follows you) work best as **surprise drops** and GM spotlights, which the research treats as far safer than announced prizes. Story and verbal feedback carry the rest: the lobby blacksmith NPC saying "you've forged your first command!" is the positive-feedback condition that *raised* interest.

The leaderboard question deserves a direct answer, because Command Forge already ranks the whole class with Gold, Silver, and Bronze stars. That's the Hanus & Fox condition. A better default is **three or four houses with cooperative totals**, where every kid's quests add to their house and the whole studio fills a shared goal (a broken bridge in the hub that gets repaired as the class completes quests), with personal bests for timed games and the individual leaderboard opt-in. That also matches the D&D party and the advice from a Minecraft STEM resource to put kids in named groups of two to four working toward quest goals together ([CCEA](https://ccea.org.uk/document/10687)). No study isolates houses versus leaderboards for kids, so this is reasoned from the adult and classroom evidence, not measured.

The strongest learning effect in this whole body of research comes from kids **making** games, not playing them. Kafai's fourth graders who spent six months programming fraction games for third graders mastered fractions in the process ([Kafai, Minds in Play](https://www.yasminkafai.com/minds-in-play)), and she frames this constructionist approach against lessons embedded in games ([Kafai PDF](https://nideffer.net/classes/270-08/misc/learning/gamesandlearning_kafai.pdf)). That points to a ladder of kid-made RPG content in rising difficulty: an NPC through NPC Forge's forms, then a named item with `/give`, then a quest (an NPC plus a command-block check and a reward), then a dungeon room with a pressure-plate trap and a dice roll, and finally a one-shot a kid runs as GM for friends, with older kids building quests that teach younger ones a command. The facilitator still matters most. Game to Grow's weekly 90-minute D&D groups for ages 10–14 credit the facilitator's framing of each challenge as skill practice ([UW iSchool](https://ischool.uw.edu/news/2023/04/phd-student-caroline-pitt-explores-gaming-educational-tool?nav=4)), so Dave's tools should include teacher-as-GM functions (a story beat, a surprise title for a kid who helped a teammate, a live event over the plaza), not just automated rewards.

## Conclusion

The popular-games data and the motivation research point to the same structure from opposite directions. Players stay for a character that grows and play in short, legible rounds; children learn best when they build things and when rewards are information, story, and surprise rather than wages. Game Forge already has the uncommon part, a single world where one profile can follow a kid through every dimension. The concrete next step is a thin shared character layer in `forge:main` (name, house, class, titles, unlocked tools, shown on a `/trigger sheet` dialog), fed by the quest completions Command Forge and NPC Forge already record, before building any new minigame. The first new games worth building are the cheap, short-round ones (Spleef or Block Drop, Hole in the Wall, Battle Box, Build Battle on `build-plots`), because each can double as a project kids design on paper and then build with the commands they learned.

The open risks are practical, not conceptual. Nobody has measured houses versus leaderboards or cross-game avatars for this age group, so Dave's playtests are the real evidence; Aeternum is the only vanilla RPG proof and it predates the 26.x JSON changes; and op level 1 for the kids still blocks the Command Forge quests that teach `/effect` and beyond. The most important design discipline is to resist the shop: no currency for lessons, nothing for sale, nothing earned that gives one kid power over another, and a pause trigger for anyone who's had enough.
